Introduction

What is Artificial Intelligence?

Artificial intelligence (AI) is the field of computer science that builds systems able to perform tasks that normally require human intelligence, such as recognizing images, understanding language, making predictions and solving problems.

The term was introduced in 1955–1956 by John McCarthy and colleagues for the Dartmouth workshop. Today AI is built into search engines, phones, social media, banking, health care and education, and it is shaping what we now call emerging digital culture: new ways of creating, learning, working and relating to each other through technology.

Key idea: AI does not “think” like a person. It detects patterns in huge amounts of data and uses them to make predictions or generate content. That is why human judgment is still essential.

🗂️

Data

Billions of texts, images and clicks created every day provide the “experience” that AI models learn from.

🧮

AI changes work, education, art and communication, and raises questions about ethics, privacy and truth.

Subtopic 1

Types of Artificial Intelligence

AI can be classified by how capable it is and by how it works. Understanding the types helps us separate today’s reality from science fiction.

Fundamental facts

  • By capability: Artificial Narrow Intelligence (ANI), Artificial General Intelligence (AGI) and Artificial Super Intelligence (ASI).
  • Only ANI exists today. Every AI system in use, including chatbots and image generators, is designed for specific tasks.
  • By functionality (a classification popularized by Arend Hintze, 2016): reactive machines, limited memory, theory of mind and self-aware AI.
  • By learning method: supervised, unsupervised and reinforcement learning, all part of machine learning.

1. Types by capability

Narrow AI (ANI) is excellent at one task, like translating, recognizing faces or recommending songs, but it cannot transfer that skill to a different field.

General AI (AGI) would be able to learn and reason across any intellectual task at a human level. It is still a research goal and does not exist.

Super AI (ASI) is a hypothetical intelligence that would surpass the best human minds in practically every area. It appears in science fiction and in debates about long-term risks.

2. Types by functionality

♟️Exists

Reactive machines

React only to the current situation. They have no memory and do not learn from the past.

Example: IBM Deep Blue, the chess computer that beat Kasparov in 1997.

🚗Exists

Limited memory

Use recent data to make decisions. Most modern AI belongs here.

Example: driver-assistance systems that track nearby cars; chatbots that use the current conversation.

🧑‍🤝‍🧑Not yet

Theory of mind

Would understand human emotions, beliefs and intentions. Researchers are still far from this.

Today: some apps detect emotions, but they do not truly understand them.

🪞Not yet

Self-aware AI

Would have consciousness and a sense of self. It is purely hypothetical.

Today: only appears in fiction and philosophy.

3. How machines learn

Supervised learning

Learns from examples that already have the correct answer (“labeled data”).

Example: an email filter trained on messages marked as spam or not spam.

Unsupervised learning

Finds hidden groups and patterns in data without labels.

Example: grouping customers with similar shopping habits.

Reinforcement learning

Learns by trial and error, receiving rewards for good decisions.

Example: AlphaGo improving by playing millions of Go games.

Real-life examples

🎬

Streaming recommendations

Platforms such as Netflix and Spotify suggest content based on what you and similar users enjoy. (Narrow AI)

🧬

AlphaFold

DeepMind’s system predicts the 3D shape of proteins with high accuracy, helping biological research. (Narrow AI)

🗣️

Voice assistants & chatbots

Siri, Alexa and generative chatbots understand and produce language, but only within what they were trained to do.

Subtopic 2

Ethics, Risks and Regulations

Powerful technology needs clear rules. This section explores the ethical principles, the main risks of AI and the laws and frameworks created to guide it.

Fundamental facts

  • Ethics asks what AI should do. Common principles are fairness, transparency, privacy, accountability, safety and human oversight.
  • Risks include bias, privacy violations, misinformation (deepfakes), “hallucinations”, job disruption and environmental cost.
  • UNESCO adopted the first global standard, the Recommendation on the Ethics of AI, in November 2021, endorsed by its 193 member states.
  • The EU AI Act (Regulation 2024/1689) entered into force on 1 August 2024 and applies in phases.

Core ethical principles

Fairness: AI should not discriminate against people because of gender, skin color, age or origin.

Transparency and explainability: people should know when they interact with AI and understand, at least in general terms, how a decision was made.

Privacy: personal data must be protected and used with consent.

Accountability: a human or organization must be responsible for what an AI system does.

Safety and human oversight: people must be able to supervise, correct or stop the system.

Main risks and real examples

⚖️

Algorithmic bias

If training data underrepresents some groups, the model may perform worse for them.

Real case: the 2018 “Gender Shades” study found that commercial face-analysis systems made far more errors for darker-skinned women (up to nearly 35%) than for lighter-skinned men (under 1%).

🔍

Hallucinations

Generative AI can produce answers that sound confident but are false or invented.

Real case: in 2023, lawyers in Mata v. Avianca (New York) were sanctioned after submitting a brief with fake court cases generated by ChatGPT.

🎭

Deepfakes & misinformation

AI can create realistic fake images, audio and video, which can be used for scams, harassment or political manipulation.

Tip: check the source, look for context and use trusted fact-checkers.

🔐

Privacy & surveillance

Models may be trained on personal data, and facial recognition can track people without consent.

Tip: never share passwords, ID numbers or private information in AI chats.

Regulations and frameworks

The EU AI Act is a risk-based law: the higher the risk an AI system poses to people’s rights and safety, the stricter the rules.

  • Unacceptable risk: banned practices, for example government “social scoring” and manipulative AI that exploits vulnerabilities.
  • High risk: AI used in hiring, education, credit scoring or critical infrastructure must meet strict requirements.
  • Limited risk: transparency duties, such as telling users they are talking to a chatbot and labeling deepfakes.
  • Minimal risk: most systems, like spam filters or video-game AI, face no extra obligations.
🌐

UNESCO (2021)

Global ethical standard focused on human rights, dignity, fairness and environmental sustainability.

🏛️

OECD AI Principles (2019)

The first intergovernmental standard on AI: inclusive growth, human-centered values, transparency, robustness and accountability.

🧰

NIST AI RMF (2023)

A voluntary framework from the United States to identify, measure and manage AI risks.

Subtopic 3

Designing Prompts with Critical Thinking

A prompt is the instruction you give to an AI model. Good prompts get better results, and critical thinking helps you decide whether to trust them.

Fundamental facts

  • Prompt: the text, question or instruction that tells a generative AI what to do.
  • Prompt engineering is the practice of writing and refining prompts to get useful, accurate output.
  • Few-shot prompting: giving the model a few examples inside the prompt improves its performance on many tasks (Brown et al., 2020).
  • Chain-of-thought prompting: asking the model to reason step by step can improve results on reasoning problems (Wei et al., 2022).
  • AI can be wrong. You are responsible for checking facts before using or sharing an answer.

Anatomy of a good prompt

🎭

Role

Who should the AI act as? “You are a biology tutor.”

📖

Context

Give background: audience, level, goal. “For first-year university students.”

🎯

Task

Say exactly what you need. “Explain photosynthesis in 5 steps.”

📐

Format & limits

Length, tone, structure. “Use a table and keep it under 150 words.”

Weak prompt vs. strong prompt

❌

Weak prompt

“Tell me about climate change.”

Too broad: the answer may be long, generic and not useful for your assignment.

✅

Strong prompt

“Act as a science teacher. Explain the three main causes of climate change to a high-school student in under 120 words, with one everyday example for each. Then list two reliable sources I can check.”

It has a role, audience, task, limits and a request for sources.

The critical-thinking checklist

1️⃣

Question the answer

Does it make sense? Is anything missing or too good to be true?

2️⃣

Verify with sources

Compare with books, journals, official websites or experts. Check that cited sources really exist.

3️⃣

Look for bias

Does the answer favor one viewpoint? Ask for other perspectives.

4️⃣

Iterate

Refine your prompt: add details, ask follow-up questions, request corrections.

5️⃣

Protect your data

Do not paste passwords, private information or other people’s data.

6️⃣

Be honest

Follow your school’s rules and give credit when AI helped with your work. Learn from the tool, don’t just copy.

Real-life examples

🎓

Studying

A student asks the AI to quiz them on a topic and explain every wrong answer, then confirms the explanations in the textbook.

💼

At work

An employee asks for a draft email in a professional tone, then edits it and checks the facts before sending.

🧑‍⚖️

A cautionary tale

The lawyers in Mata v. Avianca trusted fabricated citations without checking: critical thinking was missing.

    Pair 2 · Ethics and risks

    Basic prompt
    Tell me about AI ethics.
    Structured prompt
    Act as an AI ethics researcher. Write a clear explanation for high-school and university students of four risks of AI: bias, privacy, misinformation and hallucinations.     Responses and comparison
    Basic response (Claude)

    AI ethics is about using AI responsibly. Main issues include bias, privacy, transparency, accountability and the impact on jobs. Companies and governments are creating rules so AI is fair and safe, but the debate continues.

    Structured response (Claude)
    • Bias: Unfair results caused by skewed data or design. Case: The 2018 Gender Shades study found that commercial face-analysis systems made far more errors for darker-skinned women than for lighter-skinned men. Tip: Check whether a tool was tested on diverse groups.
    • Privacy: Personal data collected or exposed without consent. Case: In 2024 the Dutch data protection authority fined Clearview AI €30.5 million for building a facial-recognition database from scraped images. Tip: Never paste private data into AI chats.
    • Misinformation: False content that looks real, such as deepfakes. Case: In January 2024, an AI-generated voice imitating President Biden was used in robocalls to New Hampshire voters. Tip: Check the source before sharing.
    • Hallucinations: Confident answers that are false or invented. Case: In 2023, lawyers in Mata v. Avianca were sanctioned for citing court cases invented by ChatGPT. Tip: Verify every fact and citation.

    Note: I am less sure about exact figures such as fine amounts, so verify them in the original sources.

    Comparison: 2 key differences
    1. Relevance: the structured answer covers exactly the four risks requested, each with a definition, a dated real case and a tip; the basic answer only lists general topics.
    2. Verifiability: the real cases and years can be checked, and the model admits where it is less sure; the basic answer has nothing to check.

    AI ethics is about using AI responsibly. Main issues include bias, privacy, transparency, accountability and the impact on jobs. Companies and governments are creating rules so AI is fair and safe, but the debate continues.

    Prompt estructurado: Act as an AI ethics researcher. Write a clear explanation for high-school and university students of four risks of AI: bias, privacy, misinformation and hallucinations. For each risk give a definition, one real documented case with its year, and one tip to avoid it. Use short bullet points, a neutral tone, maximum 250 words, and say if you are not sure about a fact.

    Respuesta:

    • Bias: Unfair results caused by skewed data or design. Case: The 2018 Gender Shades study found that commercial face-analysis systems made far more errors for darker-skinned women than for lighter-skinned men. Tip: Check whether a tool was tested on diverse groups.
    • Privacy: Personal data collected or exposed without consent. Case: In 2024 the Dutch data protection authority fined Clearview AI €30.5 million for building a facial-recognition database from scraped images. Tip: Never paste private data into AI chats.
    • Misinformation: False content that looks real, such as deepfakes. Case: In January 2024, an AI-generated voice imitating President Biden was used in robocalls to New Hampshire voters. Tip: Check the source before sharing.
    • Hallucinations: Confident answers that are false or invented. Case: In 2023, lawyers in Mata v. Avianca were sanctioned for citing court cases invented by ChatGPT. Tip: Verify every fact and citation.

    Note: I am less sure about exact figures such as fine amounts, so verify them in the original sources.

    Par 3 · Regulaciones

    Prompt básico: What are the laws about AI?

    Respuesta:

    There is no single global AI law. The European Union has the AI Act, which sets rules by risk level. The United States has a mix of state laws and guidelines, and other countries are creating their own. International groups such as UNESCO and the OECD have also published ethical guidelines.

    Prompt estructurado: Act as a legal-tech explainer. Summarize how the EU AI Act regulates AI by risk level (unacceptable, high, limited, minimal) for a non-expert audience. Give one example of an AI system for each level, mention the year it entered into force, and compare it in two sentences with the UNESCO Recommendation on the Ethics of AI. Use a table followed by a short paragraph, and cite the official sources.

    Respuesta:

    Risk levelWhat it meansExample
    UnacceptableBanned practicesGovernment social scoring
    HighStrict requirementsAI used in hiring or credit scoring
    LimitedTransparency dutiesChatbots that must say they are AI
    MinimalNo extra obligationsSpam filters

    The EU AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689) entered into force on 1 August 2024 and applies in phases. Unlike the UNESCO Recommendation on the Ethics of AI (2021), which is a non-binding global ethical standard, the EU AI Act is a binding law that can impose fines.

    Sources: EUR-Lex, Regulation (EU) 2024/1689; UNESCO (2021).
